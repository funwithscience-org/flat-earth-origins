461 Proofs, 98 Arguments, 20 Authors

A provenance review of the flat-earth & geocentric “proof” lists

Flat-earth and geocentric “proof lists” circulate as long numbered compilations — 100 items, 200 items, 461 items. They look overwhelming by volume. This review takes one representative specimen and does the thing a numbered list is built to prevent: it sorts the claims by where they came from. Sorted that way, 461 items collapse to 98 distinct arguments, and 366 of the 461 trace to 20 named people — most of them dead, several of them contradicting each other, and two of them the movement’s own founders.

Specimen: the 461-item list at withthesun33.com/about-1, retrieved 2 August 2026, used as a representative compilation. This page reviews published claims, never a person.

The one-line thesis. Two different claims are fused in these lists — that the Earth is flat, and that it is non-rotating — and neither half is supported by any item that distinguishes it from the ordinary spinning globe. The geocentric half cites real experiments whose published results point the other way. The flat half is Victorian folk observation, answered at book length before 1905. The remainder is scripture, symbolism and misread astronomy. The list is not 461 pieces of evidence. It is 98 arguments, restated.

The Source Tree, Part 1: The People

The rhetorical power of a numbered list is that each item reads as an independent witness. 461 witnesses is a crowd. But a claim is not a witness — it has an author, and authors can be counted. When every item on this list is traced back to the publication that first put it into circulation, the crowd resolves into 20 people, working in two largely separate traditions that were never reconciled with each other, plus a body of esoteric literature whose authors were not making claims about geography at all.

The distribution is the finding. This is a movement with very few producers and very many distributors. One author — Samuel Rowbotham, writing in 1849–1865 — is behind 65 of the 461 items. One modern author, Robert Sungenis, across his three bylines, is behind 128. Between them, two men account for 42% of a list that presents itself as 461 independent proofs.

Two braided lineages

The list fuses two movements that are historically distinct, make incompatible claims, and have never merged. The zetetic tradition says the Earth is a flat plane. The Tychonian tradition says the Earth is a sphere that does not move. Every authority the list cites in its historical sections — Ptolemy, Aristotle, Tycho Brahe — held the Earth to be a globe. The list cites them against itself.

The zetetic line — and where the numbered list itself comes from

Samuel Rowbotham (“Parallax”), 1849 pamphlet → Earth Not a Globe, 1865. Origin of the Bedford Level experiment, “water finds its level,” horizon-at-eye-level, the perspective explanation of sunset, and the “8 inches per mile squared” formula — which he took from an encyclopaedia article on surveying, i.e. from the discipline that uses it to correct for the curvature he denied.

William Carpenter, One Hundred Proofs that the Earth Is Not a Globe, Baltimore, 1885. This is where the format is born. There is no numbered proof-list in Rowbotham; Carpenter condensed Rowbotham’s prose into 100 discrete numbered items, each ending “…is a proof that the Earth is not a globe.” Every list since — 100, 200, 461 — is his template.

Wilbur Glenn Voliva reprinted Carpenter’s pamphlet at Zion, Illinois in 1929 and taught it in the town’s schools. Charles K. Johnson (1972–2001) added the layer the Victorians had no use for: that the astronomers are not mistaken but lying. Eric Dubay’s 200 Proofs (2015) names Rowbotham, Carpenter, Winship, Scott and Blount’s Earth Review in 17 of its 200 items and closes the list with a Rowbotham quotation.

Sources: Schadewald, The Plane Truth; Library of Congress, The Flat Earth and its Advocates; Carpenter 1885 (Project Gutenberg); Pannofino, Genealogy 8:32 (2024).

The Tychonian line — modern geocentrism

Walter van der Kamp founded the Tychonian Society in Canada in 1971. His 1988 book De Labore Solis: Airy’s Failure Reconsidered is the earliest documented use of the phrase “Airy’s failure” — a term that does not exist in physics. Every occurrence of it traces to this movement. Bouw’s obituary of van der Kamp credits him with the coinage by name.

Gerardus Bouw (PhD astronomy, Case Western Reserve), Geocentricity, 1992 — the movement’s only credentialed astronomer. He rebuilt the model to accommodate stellar aberration, contradicting van der Kamp, and conceded in print that his model is observationally equivalent to heliocentrism and must therefore be chosen on theological rather than scientific grounds.

Robert Sungenis & Robert Bennett, Galileo Was Wrong: The Church Was Right (2006), and the 2014 film The Principle. This is the direct source of the list’s Michelson–Gale, Sagnac, Miller, Foucault and CMB material. The film’s narrator Kate Mulgrew publicly disavowed it, as did the physicists who appear in it — Lawrence Krauss, Michio Kaku, Max Tegmark, George Ellis and Julian Barbour.

Sources: Association for Biblical Astronomy; van der Kamp, De Labore Solis (1988); Bouw’s obituary of van der Kamp; Krauss, Slate, 8 April 2014.

A correction to this review’s own starting assumption

This page was scoped on a bucketing sweep that estimated Family B (the Dubay/flat-earth material) at roughly 105 items, the largest single family. The full pass gives 54 — the smallest. The geocentric material, estimated at ~95, is actually 182 across its two lanes.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. A list marketed as flat-earth evidence is, by volume, predominantly a geocentrism list — and geocentrism is a spherical-Earth position. The specimen’s largest body of material is drawn from authors who would reject its headline claim.

Evaluation Guide: How We Assess a “Proof”

Same standard as the sibling dome model review. Six principles, and one question that does most of the work.

  1. Every claim is independently verifiable. Where we cite a measurement, it is in the public record with a reference.
  2. We use the claim’s own logic against it. The strongest refutation of “experiment X shows no motion” is experiment X’s published result.
  3. We evaluate against measurement, not authority. A claim is wrong when a specific prediction fails a specific measurement.
  4. We engage the strongest version. Airy’s null result and the general-covariance argument have real content. We answer the strong form.
  5. Unfalsifiable claims are identified, not ridiculed. Scripture and symbolism are named as outside the testable domain — a methodological note, not an insult.
  6. Errors in this review should be reported. Every correction is logged in the version history — including the count correction above.
The question that settles most items

Does the claim discriminate a flat/stationary Earth from the ordinary spinning globe? A result both models predict equally — a Michelson–Morley null, “relativity allows a stationary frame,” a sunset — is not evidence for either. To count, an item must be something the flat/stationary model gets right that the globe gets wrong. On this list, nothing takes that form. That single test is why the scorecard reads 0.

B · Flat-Earth Observations (54 items · 14 arguments · Rowbotham → Carpenter → Dubay · link-out)

Horizon-at-eye-level, “water finds its level,” Bedford Level, long-range ship and lighthouse sightings. This is the Victorian zetetic canon, answered before 1905 and answered again continuously since. Per the plan, we state the governing answer and point to the standing rebuttals rather than re-deriving them.

Governing answer. Most of this family is atmospheric refraction and sight-line geometry misread as flat-plane evidence; the rest is “water is level” restated with the ambiguity in “level” doing all the work. Full point-by-point treatment: flatearth.ws — rebuttals to Dubay’s 200 Proofs.

The one that answers itself

Rowbotham’s Bedford Level result (1838) used a two-point sightline eight inches above the water — the exact configuration in which refraction over a canal produces a false null. When Alfred Russel Wallace repeated it in 1870 he changed two things: he raised the sightline to 13 feet, and he added a third marker at the midpoint. The middle marker stood high, and Wallace won the wager. Henry Yule Oldham replicated it with three poles in 1901. Modern citations of “Bedford Level” point at Rowbotham’s two-point version, and treat the court’s later voiding of the wager — a ruling about the enforceability of betting contracts — as though it reversed the measurement.

C · Scriptural Appeals (69 items · 10 arguments)

Psalms’ “the world is established, it shall not be moved,” Joshua’s long day, Job’s pillars and foundations, read as literal cosmology. Numbered scriptural proofs enter the genre with Carpenter in 1885 and are greatly expanded in the YouTube era by Rob Skiba’s biblical-cosmology teaching.

Governing verdict: ∅ OUTSIDE THE TESTABLE DOMAIN. A theological reading of scripture is not a measurement and cannot be confirmed or refuted by one. We identify this once, without ridicule, and decline to litigate exegesis — noting only that many religious traditions have read these passages non-literally for most of their history, and that the question of what the text means is not settled by this review or by any instrument.

One internal note

The list cites Isaiah 40:22 (“the circle of the earth”) and Revelation 7:1 (“the four corners of the earth”) as separate proofs, and Job 26:7 (“he hangs the earth upon nothing”) alongside the pillars-and-foundations texts. These describe different things. Read as literal cosmology they conflict with each other; read as poetry they conflict with nothing. The internal tension is a feature of the proof-text method, not of the texts.

D · Historical, Mythological & Esoteric (83 items · 18 arguments)

Tycho and Ptolemy invoked as authorities, the zodiac and Atlas as evidence, temple architecture, the axis mundi, and symbolism read as encoded cosmography. Physics-oriented rebuttals skip all of this, which is exactly why it is worth addressing. Three things are going on, and they need separating.

1. Appeal to antiquity is not measurement. That earlier cultures believed something is a fact about those cultures. And the specific authorities named here cut the other way: Plato, Aristotle, Ptolemy and Tycho Brahe all held the Earth to be a sphere. Aristarchus proposed heliocentrism in the third century BCE; Eratosthenes measured the Earth’s circumference around 240 BCE and got it roughly right. “All ancient cultures were geocentric” is both an appeal to authority and false in detail.

2. Symbol resemblance is unfalsifiable pattern-matching. A mandala has a still centre; a cathedral is oriented east; a logo is drawn on a polar azimuthal projection. None of these is a measurement, and no observation could contradict them, which is precisely the problem. The UN emblem case is instructive in reverse: the azimuthal equidistant projection is a mathematically defined projection of a sphere, used for polar navigation because it preserves distance and bearing from the centre point only. The flat-earth map that resembles it is Alexander Gleason’s 1892 chart — itself an azimuthal equidistant projection. The resemblance runs the other way.

3. The esoteric sources are not saying what they are quoted as saying. “As above, so below” is a 12th-century Latin rendering of an 8th–9th-century Arabic alchemical maxim about transmutation, whose modern currency comes from The Kybalion — a 1908 Chicago New Thought pamphlet by William Walker Atkinson. Mircea Eliade and René Guénon wrote about the axis mundi as religious symbolism, not geography; Jonathan Z. Smith later showed that even Eliade’s universality claim rests on a conflation in his ethnographic sources. The Dendera zodiac is a late-Ptolemaic ceiling (c. 50 BCE, Louvre E 13482) incorporating the Babylonian–Greek zodiac — evidence of Hellenistic astronomical transmission, and a planisphere, which is a projection of a spherical sky.

4. Tycho and Ptolemy were superseded by measurement. That is the point, not an embarrassment. Geocentric models were accurate, sophisticated, and replaced because better instruments produced data they could not fit. The same standard is being applied here.

E · Misappropriated Astronomy (73 items · 18 arguments)

Genuine observational results — CMB large-angle anomalies, quasar surveys, the Hubble tension, spacecraft anomalies — quoted as if they imply a stationary or flat Earth. The data are real. The inference is inserted without warrant, and in several cases the result has since been resolved in the opposite direction.

Careful case: the CMB “axis of evil”

This one must be represented honestly, because overclaiming here would be the review’s own worst error. The alignment is real and reproducible: the CMB quadrupole and octopole are unusually planar and their axes point in nearly the same direction. Whether this is a statistical fluke, a residual systematic, or new physics is a live question in cosmology, argued by serious people on both sides — Schwarz, Copi, Huterer and Starkman still maintain there is something to explain.

What can be said firmly is narrower, and sufficient. Land and Magueijo, who coined the phrase, revisited it in 2007 and found the significance was not robust — “no evidence” for the general model under Bayesian comparison. The WMAP team and Planck 2018 both emphasise the look-elsewhere effect, and Planck finds no corresponding anomaly in polarization.

And the geocentric reading is undercut by the anomaly’s own geometry. The axis aligns with the ecliptic and the dipole — the solar system’s plane and our own motion. That is the signature of a local contaminant: zodiacal dust, foreground residuals, scanning strategy. If the alignment means anything, the most likely thing it means is that part of the signal is not cosmological at all — which is the opposite of “the cosmos is centred on us.”

Verdict legend

REFUTED — a specific claim contradicted by a specific measurement.
STANDARD PHYSICS — the observation is real but already explained by ordinary physics; it does not discriminate.
SELF-CONTRADICTED — the claim’s own cited source, or another item on the same list, points the other way.
MISLEADING — real data, but presented so the wrong conclusion looks supported.
UNFALSIFIABLE — outside the domain of testable measurement (scripture, symbolism).
NOT DEMONSTRATED — asserted, but the argument to the conclusion is not actually made.

Same six-verdict scheme as the dome model review, applied to a claim list instead of a prediction list.

How this review is built

Every verdict cites a public source or a reproducible calculation. We engage the strongest form of each argument. Unfalsifiable claims are named rather than mocked. Duplicate items are grouped and scored once per distinct argument, so the “461” headline does not inflate the work. Provenance attributions are traced to a specific publication with a date; where an attribution could not be established, the item is recorded as untraced rather than guessed.

Known limits of this pass. 95 of 461 items could not be traced to a named origin. Cluster boundaries involve judgement: a handful of items could defensibly sit in an adjacent cluster, which would move counts by a few units without changing any verdict. Carpenter’s 1885 pamphlet is the earliest numbered proof-list identified, not provably the first — his own earlier works were not available to check.
